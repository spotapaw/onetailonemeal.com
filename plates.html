Where the vegetables come from

Rescued before
they are thrown away

The principle is simple: use what would otherwise be wasted.
We will only take vegetables that are still safe to eat.

Start here

Wholesale markets

Cairo's big wholesale markets, starting with Obour, sell misshapen, small and bruised vegetables cheaply, especially at the end of the day. One early pickup can cover a whole day of plates.

Later, for bulk

Farms

At harvest, farms grade out "ugly" crops that shops won't take. Once the truck is running, the plan is to build direct deals with farms for larger volumes.

Partners

Supermarkets

Supermarkets have small mixed lots of unsold stock. We see them as partners who help tell the story, not as the main supply.

Only leftovers

Food-rescue groups

Groups like food banks already collect surplus food, and it rightly goes to people first. We would only ask for what is no longer fit for people.

What we accept, and what we turn away

  • Misshapen or odd-sized
  • Small or end-of-day stock
  • Bruised, with the bruise cut out
  • Green or sprouting
  • Soft, mouldy or rotten
  • Peelings and kitchen scraps
How a plate is made

Six steps from
crate to plate

This is the recipe we plan to test first.
It uses no special machines, just a kitchen, a mould and an oven.

  1. Peel and cook. Every vegetable is washed, peeled where needed, and cooked until soft. Nothing goes in raw.
  2. Mash. The cooked vegetables are mashed or blended smooth, with no salt.
  3. Bind. A little plain flour or vegetable starch is mixed in, so the plate holds together instead of cracking as it dries.
  4. Press. The dough is pressed into a round mould, about as thick as a pencil.
  5. Bake low and slow. The plates bake at a low heat, then stay in the cooling oven until they are completely dry. A dry plate is a strong plate.
  6. Store and use within a week. Plates are kept airtight and made in small batches, so none of them go mouldy in Cairo's heat.
Safe for dogs and cats

If it isn't safe,
it never goes in

Every plate will be eaten, so every ingredient has to be safe for dogs and cats.

Never used

OnionGarlicLeekChivesGrapes and raisinsChocolateXylitol (sugar-free sweetener)Macadamia nutsNutmegRaw dough

Always

Fully cookedPeeled where neededNo salt or sugarNo coatings or dyesNo plastic linings

Cats mostly ignore plates, and that is fine. A plate left behind breaks down naturally within days.

The first test bake

Twenty plates,
tested on camera

Before a single plate goes on the street, we will test the recipe in a kitchen and film every step.

  1. Bake three versions. Two with different binders and one with vegetables only, about 20 plates in all.
  2. Add a real meal. Time how long each plate holds wet food before it softens and breaks.
  3. Leave one out for a week. See which version stays dry and free of mould longest.
  4. Post the result. Whether the plates hold up or fall apart, you will see it.

What the test will measure

  • Strength. How long a plate holds a wet meal. Street animals eat fast, so it only needs to last one meal.
  • Shelf life. How long a bone-dry plate keeps in Cairo's heat, so we know the right batch size.
  • Speed. How many plates a kitchen can make a day, and when to add a press and dryer.
  • Cost. The real cost of each plate, which we will publish after the test.
Until the plates are ready

Still nothing
left on the street

Bread for dogs

A round of plain local bread makes a plate that dogs eat. It is cheap, sold by every bakery, and leaves nothing behind.

Steel dishes for cats

Cats ignore bread, so they get a stainless steel dish that the crew collects and washes after the meal.

Natural plates when we can't wait

Where the crew has to move on, untreated palm-leaf plates or uncoated sugarcane bowls, checked free of "forever chemical" coatings.
